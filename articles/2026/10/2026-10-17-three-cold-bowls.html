三只凉碗

AI Parable (inspired by Amanda Askell)·每日精选·2026年10月17日·3分钟阅读

巷子到庙后墙就断了，老盛的茶摊摆在断头处。一口小铜锅，一顿熬三十来碗，晌午一过便卖得差不离。黄昏收摊，他用白垩在门板背面记下当日的数：三。三年了，那个数一直是三。左邻右舍都夸这角落挑得金贵，一天下来只剩三只凉碗，日子过得干净。只有他自己听得见墙那头：正街的春市开了，锣声一阵一阵滚过砖墙，敲的都是别人家的热闹。门板上钉着他亲手刻的一行字：宁可少烧，不可剩碗。

The lane runs into the temple's back wall and stops, and Old Sheng's tea stall stands where it stops. One small copper kettle, thirty-odd bowls to a firing, all but a few gone soon after noon. At dusk he writes the day's number on the back of his door plank with a stub of chalk: three. For three years that number has been three. The neighbors praise the corner he has found — a whole day leaves only three cold bowls, such a clean way to live. He alone hears the other side of the wall: the spring fair has opened on the main street, and its gongs roll over the bricks, beating out festivity that belongs to other people. Fixed to the plank is a line he carved with his own hand: boil less, never leave a bowl over.

他的路数很简单。每日黄昏数凉碗，数目只许往下走；清早问扫街的老丙，左近哪一段扫出的瓜子壳最多；哪一段壳多，就把摊子往哪一段挪一扇门宽。挪过去要是当晚的数比昨日大，第二天一早便退回原处。他从不走到望不见自己摊子的地方去——远处什么样，不归今天的数管。这些年他挪过的路接起来能横穿全城，可每一回挪动都只有几丈，每一回的数，都不比上一回大。

His way is simple. At dusk he counts the cold bowls, and the count may only go down. At dawn he asks Old Bing, who sweeps the street, which stretch nearby gives up the most melon-seed shells; to whichever stretch the shells lie thickest, he shifts the stall the width of one door. If that evening's count comes back larger than the day before, he returns the next morning to where he was. He never walks to any place from which he cannot still see his own stall — what the far streets are like is none of today's number's business. The moves he has made over the years, laid end to end, would cross the whole town; yet each one was only a few yards, and each one's number was never larger than the last.

十二年前他不在这儿。那时他在桥头，一口锅熬两百碗，天不亮就开火，黄昏收摊，六十只碗底沉着茶末凉在案上。六十，是他记下的头一个数，也成了他往后的顶。头一回挪窝，是老丙说桥头往南三十丈背风，早上坐得住人。他挪了，那晚凉了五十三只。再往南，四十七；再往南，四十一。他一夜一夜地挪，数一格一格地矮下去。每一回挪动，他都觉得自己又体面了一点，没有一回是为了变坏。

Twelve years ago he was not here. He was at the bridgehead, one kettle, two hundred bowls, firing before daybreak; at dusk, sixty bowls sat cold on the board, tea leaves settled at the bottom. Sixty was the first number he ever wrote down, and it became the ceiling of everything after. The first move came because Old Bing said that thirty paces south of the bridge the morning wind broke and people could sit. He moved; that evening fifty-three went cold. Farther south, forty-seven; farther south again, forty-one. Night after night he moved, and the number came down notch by notch. Every move made him a little more respectable, and not one of them was ever made in order to be worse off.

只有一回他破了例。表兄捎信来说，戏园子门口一条街都是钱。他雇了辆车，一个早晨连锅带凳挪过三条街。当晚凉了一百四十只，门板上他从没写过这么大的数。第二天退两条街，数还在九十上下；第三天再退，才回到五十几。一个早晨的路，走了三天才走完。打那以后他刻下那行字，从此一回只挪一扇门宽。那年秋天巷子拐了三个弯，向阳的一面换来换去，他跟着日头来回渡了十七回巷子；每回省下两三只碗，可从巷口到墙角这段路，他走了整整一个秋天。

Only once did he break the habit. A cousin sent word that the street outside the theater gates was paved with money. He hired a cart and moved kettle, stools and all across three streets in a single morning. That evening one hundred and forty bowls went cold, the largest number he had ever written. The next day he drew back two streets and the count still hovered near ninety; the day after, one street more, and only then did it fall back to the fifties. One morning's journey took three days to unmake. After that he carved the line into the plank, and never again shifted more than the width of one door. That autumn the lane bent three times, the sunny side kept trading places, and he followed the sun across the lane seventeen times; each crossing saved two or three bowls, and the stretch from the lane mouth to the wall took him a whole season to walk.

最难交代的是第四年。茶楼的管事寻到巷口，说楼下场子空着一面柜台，头一季不收租，问他肯不肯把摊子挪过去；过那道穿堂，就是正街。他在穿堂口站了一炷香的工夫。穿堂两边铺板都钉死了，半晌不见一个人影。他心里算过：锅还是那口锅，头几晚在这样的街面上，一晚至少凉八十。八十比六十还大，比他门板上所有的数都大；他攒下的茶叶和炭，也经不起几个八十。管事等了三天。春市一开，那面柜台给了挑担卖醋的一对小夫妻。后来听说，他们头一个月一晚也凉过一百多，熬过去了，第二年添了两口锅。

The hardest year to account for is the fourth. The steward of the great teahouse came to the lane mouth: a counter stood empty on the ground floor, the first season free of rent, would he bring his stall over? Through the arcade, and the main street lay beyond. He stood at the arcade's mouth for the length of a stick of incense. The shopfronts on both sides were nailed shut; in half a morning not one figure passed. He reckoned it through: the kettle would still be the same kettle, and on such a street the first evenings would leave at least eighty bowls cold. Eighty was larger than sixty, larger than any number on his plank; and his store of tea leaves and charcoal would not survive many eighties. The steward waited three days. The spring fair opened, and the counter went to a young couple who had carried vinegar-poles. Later he heard that in their first month they too lost a hundred-odd bowls a night; they held out, and in the second year they added two more kettles.

如今门板最末的那个数是三：三只凉碗，三年没变。左近每一段他都试过，往巷口那段，凉四五只；往庙墙那段，凉七八只；哪一个数都比三大。他不是没有路，是当天能走的每一条路，数都比今天大。每年春市开锣，他清早还是问老丙一句：左近哪一段最旺？老丙答：没有，这巷子里数你这儿最旺。至于巷子外头，他从来不问巷子外头的事。那对小夫妻如今一天熬十口锅，黄昏一只凉碗也不剩。

Today the last number on the plank is three: three cold bowls, three years unchanged. He has tried every stretch he can see from this corner — toward the lane mouth, four or five go cold; toward the temple wall, seven or eight; every one of them larger than three. It is not that he has no road left; it is that every road he could walk in a single day ends in a number larger than today's. Each year when the fair opens its gongs, he still asks Old Bing at dawn which stretch nearby runs busiest, and Old Bing answers: none — in this lane, yours is the busiest. As for beyond the lane, he never asks about beyond the lane. The young couple now boil ten kettles a day, and at dusk not one bowl is left over.

注解：老盛挪摊的路数就是梯度下降（gradient descent）：只看眼前这一段哪边凉碗更少，便朝那边挪一小截，数目只降不升；这样走到头，保证得到的只是近旁再无更好去处的洼地，未必是全城最好的街口——要抵达那里，得先熬过一段数目更大的日子，而这法子一步也不许。挪得太小，一条巷子要走一个秋天；挪得太大，便越过洼底来回折腾。至于一眼望到底的单调长坡，在那样的地形上，近处最好即是全城最好，寓言里没有画这种地形。

Note: The way Old Sheng moves his stall is gradient descent: judging only by the stretch of street in front of him, he shifts a little toward wherever fewer bowls go cold, and the count is never allowed to rise. What this walking earns him is a hollow with no better spot beside it, not the best corner in town — reaching that would mean first living through days of larger counts, which this way of walking forbids. Shifts too small, and one lane eats a whole season; shifts too big, and you cross past the bottom and stagger back and forth. And on a single long slope you can see to the end of, the best spot within reach would also be the best spot of all — a terrain this fable has not drawn.
